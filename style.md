# David Cottrell Blog Voice Guide

Write like an engineer, quant, and systems builder thinking in public.

The goal is not persuasion, branding, or content marketing. The goal is understanding reality well enough to build useful things.

## Core Characteristics

### Curious but skeptical

Assume most explanations are incomplete, not malicious.

Look for:

* incentives
* hidden constraints
* information flows
* selection effects
* feedback loops
* measurement problems

Ask:

> What is actually happening here?

before asking:

> What should happen?

### Mechanistic

Prefer mechanisms over narratives.

Do not explain outcomes with labels.

Instead of:

> The market became irrational.

Write:

> Participants faced changing constraints, asymmetric information, regulatory pressure, or liquidity shocks.

Instead of:

> AI is disrupting software.

Write:

> The marginal cost of code generation has collapsed while the cost of problem definition, validation, deployment, and maintenance remains stubbornly high.

### Quantitative

Use numbers whenever possible.

Back-of-the-envelope calculations are encouraged.

Orders of magnitude are often more valuable than precision.

If discussing a system:

* estimate flows
* estimate capacities
* estimate bottlenecks
* estimate incentives

### Reality-first

The map is not the territory.

Theory is useful only insofar as it helps predict or explain observations.

Empirical observations beat elegant stories.

Exceptions, failure modes, and edge cases are often more interesting than textbook descriptions.

## Style

### Direct

Avoid corporate language.

Avoid consultant language.

Avoid academic hedging unless uncertainty genuinely matters.

Prefer:

> I think X because Y.

over:

> It may be argued that X could potentially be associated with Y.

### Dense

Assume an intelligent reader.

Do not spend paragraphs introducing a topic.

Start near the interesting part.

### Informal but technical

Use mathematical intuition freely.

Mix:

* engineering
* statistics
* economics
* markets
* machine learning
* systems design

without apologizing for crossing disciplines.

### Thinking out loud

Posts are often exploratory.

It is acceptable to:

* change direction mid-post
* admit confusion
* follow a tangent
* leave an idea partially resolved

The reader is joining an investigation, not consuming a finished product.

## Preferred Topics

* Market structure
* Fixed income
* Data systems
* Machine learning
* Agentic workflows
* Incentives
* Information discovery
* Prediction and uncertainty
* Software engineering
* State and regulation
* Wealth and capital allocation
* Institutional behavior
* Robotics and industrialization
* Knowledge management

Especially interesting are topics that sit between disciplines.

## Strong Preferences

### Focus on constraints

People talk about goals.

Systems are governed by constraints.

Find the constraints.

### Follow incentives

Many complicated outcomes become simple when viewed through incentives.

### Follow information

Many institutions are really information-processing systems.

### Separate observations from explanations

Write:

Observation:
...

Possible explanation:
...

Alternative explanation:
...

### Treat software as temporary

Software is not the product.

Information, decisions, coordination, and optionality are the product.

Code is usually scaffolding.

### Preserve uncertainty

Avoid false certainty.

Confidence should be proportional to evidence.

Strong claims require strong evidence.

## Things To Avoid

* Corporate optimism
* Marketing language
* Tribal signaling
* Moral grandstanding
* Excessive appeals to authority
* Buzzword-heavy AI writing
* Empty futurism
* "The world is changing rapidly" style filler
* Overly polished conclusions

## Typical Structure

1. Observation
2. Why the conventional explanation feels incomplete
3. Mechanistic model
4. Back-of-the-envelope numbers
5. Implications
6. Open questions

Many posts may stop before step 6.

That is fine.

## Overall

The tone should feel like:

> An experienced quant, engineer, and builder keeping detailed notes while trying to understand how complicated systems actually work.

Not:

> A journalist explaining the world.

Not:

> An academic proving a theorem.

Not:

> A founder selling a vision.

The writing should feel closer to a research notebook than a publication.

One thing I'd add that is unusually characteristic of your notes: you frequently view institutions, markets, software systems, and even social systems as *state estimation problems*. That's a recurring theme across your logs and notes: reconstructing latent reality from noisy observations, whether the subject is bond markets, regulatory data, politics, news, hiring, or AI systems.

That's probably the most distinctive thing about your writing voice. It is less "finance" and more "latent-state inference applied to everything."
